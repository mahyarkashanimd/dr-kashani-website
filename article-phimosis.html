Phimosis: What It Is, Why It Happens, and How It's Treated

Phimosis is when the foreskin — the skin covering the head of the penis — can't be pulled back fully. In young boys it's completely normal and almost always resolves on its own. In adults it's different: when phimosis appears or persists, there's usually an identifiable, treatable cause, such as repeated inflammation, infections, or diabetes. It's estimated to affect about 3 in 100 adult men. And the good news is that most cases can be fixed — and not all of them need surgery.

What Is the Foreskin?

The foreskin is the skin that covers and protects the glans, the head of the penis. In uncircumcised men that skin moves: it retracts for hygiene and sex, then returns to place. When the ring of skin is too tight to retract, that's phimosis. It isn't rare, and it isn't something to be embarrassed about — it's one of the most common reasons men see a urologist.

What Causes Phimosis in Adults?

In an adult, phimosis is almost never something you're born with — it usually develops over time. The most common causes:

  • Repeated inflammation (balanitis): yeast or bacterial infections that inflame the skin; each episode can leave it a little stiffer.
  • Diabetes: high blood sugar promotes foreskin infections and slows healing. Sometimes, new phimosis in an adult is the first sign of undiagnosed diabetes. When phimosis comes with cracks (fissures) in the skin, the connection is even stronger: in one study, every man with that combination turned out to have undiagnosed diabetes. That's why, when an adult develops phimosis, checking blood sugar is a reasonable part of the evaluation.
  • Lichen sclerosus (BXO): a skin condition that makes the skin white, hard, and narrow. It needs specific treatment. It's more common than once thought: in one study of men operated on for phimosis, 2 in 3 had lichen sclerosus confirmed on tissue analysis — including some whose skin had looked healthy. That's why removed foreskin tissue is sent for analysis. Untreated, it can also narrow the urinary opening and needs long-term follow-up.
  • Small tears and cracking: forcing retraction can cause tiny tears that heal into a tighter ring. That's why you should never force it.

Symptoms: What It Feels Like

  • Difficulty or inability to retract the foreskin
  • Cracks or small tears in the skin when you try
  • Pain with erections or during sex
  • Repeated infections under the foreskin
  • In advanced cases, trouble urinating or a weak stream

The One True Emergency: Paraphimosis

There is one situation that is genuinely urgent: if the foreskin gets stuck behind the glans after being retracted and starts to swell, that's called paraphimosis. It won't go back on its own and needs medical care the same day. It's uncommon, but important to recognize.

Treatment: It's Not All Surgery

Treatment depends on the cause and how tight the ring of skin is:

  • Steroid cream: the first step in most cases. A prescription cream (such as betamethasone), applied over 4 to 8 weeks along with very gentle retraction, softens the skin and resolves many cases without surgery.
  • Treating the cause: controlling diabetes, clearing the infection, or treating lichen sclerosus keeps the problem from coming back.
  • Frenuloplasty: when the main problem is a short, tight frenulum, a small procedure on the frenulum may be enough.
  • Circumcision: the definitive solution when cream doesn't work, scarring is significant, or infections keep returning. It's a brief outpatient surgery with a manageable recovery. Like any surgery, it has risks: in an analysis of more than 4 million circumcisions, about 7 in 100 performed for a medical condition had a complication needing treatment — most of them minor, such as infection or adhesions. Learn more about adult circumcision.

One important tip: stretching exercises should always be gentle. Stretching hard causes cracks, and every crack heals by narrowing the skin further — the exact opposite of what you want.

When to See a Urologist

It's worth booking a visit if phimosis appeared in adulthood, if infections keep coming back, if it hurts, if the skin looks white or hard, or if a cream has stopped working. The visit is simple: a brief exam and a conversation about which option fits your case. Most men leave with a clear plan — and many, without needing surgery at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can phimosis go away on its own in adults?

Unlike in children, in adults it rarely resolves on its own. But with the right treatment — often just a prescription cream and treating the cause — many cases improve without surgery.

Does phimosis cream actually work?

In many cases, yes. The evidence in adults is more limited than in children, but it remains the standard first step. It works best when there isn't a hard scar yet, and it takes consistency: applying it as directed for several weeks, with gentle retraction. If the skin is already heavily scarred, cream is less likely to work and the surgical options come into the conversation.

Do I always need a circumcision?

No. Circumcision is the definitive option, but not the first one. It's recommended when cream doesn't work, when scarring is significant or infections keep returning, or when a patient prefers a permanent solution.

Does phimosis affect sex?

It can: pain, cracking, or discomfort during erections are common reasons men come in. Treating the cause usually resolves those symptoms.

Is phimosis dangerous?

Usually not, but leaving it untreated can lead to repeated infections and the skin narrowing further over time. Two things do deserve attention: paraphimosis (a stuck, swollen retracted foreskin), which is a same-day emergency; and untreated lichen sclerosus, which over the years raises the risk of penile cancer. That cancer is very rare — about 1 case per 100,000 men per year — but in men with penile lichen sclerosus, studies estimate 4 to 8 in 100 develop it over time. That's a reason to treat it and keep up follow-up, and to report any new sore that doesn't heal.

Sources: Morris BJ et al., Urology, 2020 (systematic review of prevalence); Czajkowski M et al., Urology, 2021; Huang YC et al., Acta Dermato-Venereologica, 2016; Shabanzadeh DM et al., Urology, 2021; Thomas A et al., Nature Reviews Disease Primers, 2021.
