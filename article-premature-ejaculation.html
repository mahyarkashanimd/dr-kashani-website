How to Last Longer: A Urologist's Guide to Premature Ejaculation

Premature ejaculation means ejaculating sooner than you want to, with little sense of control over when it happens — and feeling frustrated by it. It is the most common sexual concern men report: up to 1 in 3 men say it happens to them at some point, and about 5 in 100 meet the strict medical definition. It is not a character flaw, and it is not something you simply have to live with. There are techniques you can start at home tonight, and effective medical options beyond those. This guide walks through both, in order.

What counts as premature ejaculation?

Doctors describe two patterns:

  • Lifelong: it has been this way since your first sexual experiences. Ejaculation typically happens within about 1 to 2 minutes of penetration, often sooner.
  • Acquired: you used to last longer, and it changed. Time dropped to around 3 minutes or less, or clearly less than before.

But the stopwatch is not the whole story. The working definition has three parts: it happens sooner than you want, you feel little control over it, and it bothers you. If all three are true, it counts — whatever the clock says. For context, when researchers gave 500 couples a stopwatch, the median time from penetration to ejaculation was 5.4 minutes, and the range ran from under a minute to 44 minutes. "Normal" is a wide range; control is the real issue.

Why it happens

  • Lifelong PE is mostly biology: how the brain's serotonin system regulates ejaculation, plus natural sensitivity. It can run in families.
  • Acquired PE usually has a trigger: erectile dysfunction (rushing before the erection fades), prostate inflammation or infection, thyroid problems, anxiety, stress, a new relationship, or going a long time between sexual encounters.
  • The ED loop: worrying about losing the erection makes a man rush; rushing makes him finish faster; that makes the worry worse. When erections are part of the picture, treating them often improves control too.

Techniques to try first, step by step

These are the first-line behavioral techniques in the urology guidelines. They cost nothing and have no side effects — but they are skills, and most men need a few weeks of practice.

1. The stop-start technique
During sex — or while practicing on your own — when you feel ejaculation getting close, stop all stimulation completely. Wait until the urge fades, usually about 30 seconds, then start again. Repeat the cycle 3 or 4 times before letting yourself finish. Practicing solo first makes it easier to recognize the point of no return.

2. The squeeze technique
When you feel close, squeeze the penis firmly just below the head for about 10 to 20 seconds, until the urge passes. Wait about 30 seconds, then continue. Either you or your partner can do the squeeze.

3. Ease off the accelerator
Rushing and fast, high-friction movement raise arousal the fastest. Slow down, pause, or change positions when you feel things building too quickly — and breathe slowly while you pause. This sounds simple; in combination with stop-start, it is how control is actually built.

4. Condoms
A standard condom reduces sensitivity slightly, and thicker "extended pleasure" condoms reduce it more. For some men, that small difference is enough.

5. Take the edge off beforehand
Ejaculating an hour or two before sex — for example, by masturbating — makes the next round slower for many men. It works on the same principle as the "second round" effect described below.

Why is the first round so much faster?

This is one of the most common questions men search for, and the pattern is real. After a break — several days without sex, a new partner, built-up anticipation — arousal and sensitivity are at their highest, so the first round is the fastest for most men. The second round comes after the refractory period (the recovery window after ejaculation), when arousal builds more slowly, so it typically lasts longer. If the first round is quick but you are satisfied with how things go overall, that pattern by itself is common and usually not a medical problem. If every round is fast and it frustrates you, that is the pattern worth working on — the techniques above are aimed at exactly that.

Numbing sprays and creams

Topical anesthetics are a first-line treatment in the guidelines — for many men, the simplest effective step. They contain lidocaine, prilocaine, or both, and mildly reduce sensation in the head of the penis. How to use them:

  • Apply a small amount to the head of the penis about 10 to 15 minutes before sex (follow the product's directions).
  • Before intercourse, wipe off the excess — or use a condom — so the numbing does not transfer to your partner.
  • Do not over-apply: too much numbness can dull your own pleasure and make it harder to keep an erection.

Sprays are available over the counter; stronger creams are prescription.

Prescription options

When techniques and sprays are not enough, medication is the next step. One fact first: there is no FDA-approved drug for premature ejaculation in the United States. The oral options below are prescribed off-label — a common, guideline-supported practice.

  • Daily SSRIs: certain antidepressants have a well-known side effect — delayed ejaculation — and urologists use that effect to treat PE. They take about 1 to 2 weeks to reach full effect. Your doctor will review your mood history, other medications, and side effects (which can include nausea, fatigue, and lower libido).
  • On-demand medication: instead of a daily pill, some medications can be taken just a few hours before sex. All of them are off-label in the US and are chosen case by case.
  • ED medication: if erections are unreliable too, treating the ED (with sildenafil or tadalafil, for example) often improves ejaculatory control as well.
  • Combination: the guidelines note that behavioral techniques plus medication often work better than either one alone.

Things to be careful about

  • Surgery and filler injections for PE are considered experimental by the AUA/SMSNA guideline and are not standard care.
  • Tramadol is sometimes mentioned online; it is not a first-line choice because of dependence and interaction risks.
  • Websites selling prescription PE medication without a prescription: if a site will sell it to you without any evaluation, the product's quality and safety are not checked.
  • Alcohol may slow things slightly, but it works against your erection. It is not a strategy.

When to see a urologist

Book a visit if:

  • It is new, or suddenly worse than it used to be — that can point to ED, a prostate problem, or a thyroid issue worth checking.
  • Erections are also a problem.
  • You have burning, pelvic discomfort, or urinary symptoms (possible prostatitis).
  • You have practiced the techniques for a few months without progress.
  • It is causing real frustration or affecting your relationship.

The visit is straightforward: a conversation, a focused exam, and sometimes lab work (for example, thyroid or prostate checks when indicated). Most men leave with a plan that starts simple.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a man supposed to last?

There is no single number. In the best-known stopwatch study — 500 couples across five countries — the median time from penetration to ejaculation was 5.4 minutes, with a range from about half a minute to 44 minutes. "Too fast" is not really a stopwatch number: it is ejaculating sooner than you want, with little control, and being bothered by it.

Is there a pill for premature ejaculation?

Not an approved one in the United States — no drug has FDA approval specifically for PE. Doctors do prescribe SSRIs off-label, daily or on-demand, because delayed ejaculation is a known effect of that drug class.

Do numbing sprays actually work?

Yes. Topical anesthetics are a first-line treatment in the AUA/SMSNA guideline. Timing is what makes them work: apply a small amount about 10 to 15 minutes before sex, then wipe off the excess or use a condom so your partner does not go numb.

Can premature ejaculation be cured for good?

Acquired PE often improves a great deal when the trigger is treated — ED, prostate inflammation, or a thyroid problem, for example. Lifelong PE is usually managed rather than cured outright, but "managed" can mean reliable control: with techniques, sprays, or medication, most men get to a point where it no longer runs their sex life.

Why do I finish fast the first time but last longer the second time?

Built-up arousal. After time away from sex, sensitivity and excitement are at their peak, so the first round is the fastest. After ejaculation, the refractory period slows arousal down, so the second round typically lasts longer. It is a common pattern — and the main reason ejaculating an hour or two beforehand helps some men.
